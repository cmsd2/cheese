# Inoculation percentages and culture forms

**Source:** Cheese Forum thread (cheeseforum.org), Dec 2010. Primary contributor: *linuxboy*.

A Q&A-style thread breaking down what "culture" means in cheese recipes, the units they're sold in, and how much to use.

## Cultures in one paragraph

To make cheese you need bacteria. They come as **mesophilic** (low-temp) or **thermophilic** (higher-temp) strains, sold in three forms:

| Form | What it is | Concentration |
|---|---|---|
| Bulk / mother starter | Fresh bacteria grown up in milk (buttermilk starter, clabber, mother starter) | Baseline — dosed by volume |
| **DVI / DVS** (Direct-Vat Inoculation/Set) | Freeze-dried, highly concentrated powder | ~50× bulk (one post says 10×; 50× is closer to industry) |
| Frozen pellet | Frozen concentrated pellets | Very high, similar to DVI |

You match the **number of viable bacteria** to the milk, regardless of form — concentration changes, dose changes.

## The 1–2 % rule

Recipes are typically written as a **percentage by volume of bulk starter** added to the milk:

- **1.0 %** — Tommes, soft continental styles, anything draining at high pH and acidifying in the mould
- **1.0–1.5 %** — Goudas, most Spanish/continental styles
- **1.5 %** — Hard Italian styles, semi-lactic curds
- **1.5–2.0 %** — Most workhorse hard cheeses
- **2.0 %** — Cheddar and pasta filata (cheddared curd)
- **2.0–3.0 %** — Cultured mozzarella
- **1.5–4.0 %** — Lactic curds (fast-set vs slow-set methods differ)

Danisco's general advice: **start at 1 %** and adjust from there.

## DCU — converting bulk % to freeze-dried dose

Danisco's conversion: **1 % bulk equivalent = 6.25 DCU per 100 L** of milk (a DCU is sized to give the bacteria count of a bulk-starter equivalent).

So at **1 %**, one DCU inoculates roughly **16 L** of milk.

Scaling is linear *for the same percentage*. The confusion that came up in the thread (25 DCU "should be 375 L" but a 25 DCU pack was being used in 500 L) is resolved by noting that the larger vat was being run at a **lower effective percentage** plus an extended ripening — letting the bacteria do one more doubling makes up the count. So:

> Percentage and pack size aren't independent — a larger vat per pack means longer ripening before rennet.

Pack DCU values are **printed on the package**. Danisco and Chr. Hansen between them hold ~80 % of the cheese-culture market.

## Why the percentage differs by style

It is **not** mainly about flavour intensity — it's about:

1. **Make time.** Higher % = faster acidification = shorter make. Cheddar uses 2 % to avoid a 16-hour make.
2. **Acidity curve match.** The starter % is tuned so target pH hits coincide with the texture/moisture decisions of that style.
3. **Curd moisture target.** Temperature, stir, curd size, and starter % together drive curd to the right moisture *and* the right pH at the same time.

A higher % **can** give more flavour, but if you bump it you must:

- Re-time the whole make around **pH, not the clock**.
- Accept that "faster" usually trades against quality. Bulk producers do it for cost; artisans rarely should.

## Runaway acidity

The phrase "racing against the clock" in cheese-making means **acidity arriving before the curd has reached its moisture target**. With proper starter dosing and a pH meter it shouldn't happen — but it can with:

- Raw milk wild strains whose pH curve you don't know
- Inattention — e.g. realising pH is 6.1 when you expected 6.3

If acidity is *behind* the moisture target, that's fine: stop stirring, let curds settle, let them acidify in the vat. If acidity is *ahead*, your options narrow.

## Make decisions by pH, not time

Four pH checkpoints drive the make:

1. **0.1 pH drop after culture-in** — ripening is done, add rennet
2. **Drain pH** — when to drain whey / start pressing
3. **Brine / salt pH** — when to salt or move to brine
4. **Aging pH** — entry pH for the cave

Rennet acts fastest at pH ~5.4 and 42 °C; it gets dramatically slower at higher pH or lower temp (3 min at pH 5.5 vs 15 min at pH 6.5).

## Texture and the calcium story

Texture in pressed cheeses comes from how much **colloidal calcium** stays bound in the casein matrix vs solubilised by acid. Two control points:

- **Drain (knit) pH** — when whey leaves the curd
- **Brine pH** — when the wheel enters brine

At pH 5.2 calcium and phosphorus solubilisation hit equilibrium, so most cheeses are brined at 5.4–5.5 (acidity drifts down to ~5.2 during/after brining, giving the desired texture).

More residual calcium → less crumbly; less calcium → more crumbly.

## Practical takeaways for the bench

- Start any new style at **1 %** bulk equivalent unless the recipe demands otherwise.
- For our typical small batches, **measure by pH not minutes** whenever possible.
- Know your culture's **DCU rating** and milk-volume target from the packet; back-calculate to a teaspoon dose if needed.
- If a recipe calls for a specific Danisco product (e.g. "MM"), don't take it as gospel — it's often a generic stand-in.
- Most home recipes won't give bulk %; you'll need to tweak based on outcome.
