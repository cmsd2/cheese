# Affinage

The art of ageing cheese — what happens between pressing and eating, and how to manage it. Reference companion to [calculations.md](calculations.md), [milk.md](milk.md), and [flavour.md](flavour.md).

## What affinage is

**Affinage** (French *affiner*, "to refine") is the active process of ageing cheese under controlled conditions to develop its final flavour, texture, and rind character. It is the **third phase of cheese-making** — after milk handling (acidification) and curd handling (forming and pressing), the cheese needs months of patient curating.

In commercial dairies the *affineur* is a specialist, separate from the cheese-maker. At home, you do both jobs.

## What happens biochemically

Three parallel processes drive the chemical change:

| Process | Substrate | Outcome | Time profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glycolysis** | Residual lactose → lactic acid → eventually CO₂, acetate, propionate | Acid develops first, then breaks down further | Weeks 1–4 mostly; some continues |
| **Proteolysis** | Casein → peptides → free amino acids | Texture softens; savoury, brothy, nutty notes | Months 1–12+ ; main flavour driver |
| **Lipolysis** | Triglycerides → free fatty acids → methyl ketones, lactones | Sharp, peppery, fruity, soapy notes | Slow; pronounced from month 3+ |

(See [flavour.md](flavour.md) for how to target specific outcomes.)

The enzymes responsible:
- **Residual rennet (chymosin)** — keeps cleaving casein for the life of the cheese
- **Bacterial proteases and lipases** from the starter culture — active even after the cells die
- **Surface microbiome** — moulds and yeasts on the rind also contribute, especially for surface-ripened styles

## Environmental requirements

| Variable | Target for most aged cheese | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | 10–15 °C (50–59 °F) | Slow enough for graceful flavour development; warm enough for enzymes to act |
| **Humidity** | 75–95 % (varies by style — see method table below) | Prevents drying out / cracking while limiting unwanted mould pressure |
| **Air circulation** | Gentle, not stagnant | Removes excess moisture from rind, distributes microbial activity |
| **Light** | Dark or low | UV light degrades fat and discolours rind |
| **Cleanliness** | Wipe-down every few months | Prevents cross-contamination from accumulated debris |
| **Stability** | All of the above held steady | Cheese dislikes cycling — daily temp swings of >2 °C affect rind |

### Home setups

Most home cheese-makers use one of:

1. **Wine fridge / cheese fridge** — purpose-built, holds 8–18 °C reliably. Native humidity is usually low (30–50 %) so a **humidity micro-cave** is needed.
2. **Humidity micro-cave inside a regular fridge** — sealed plastic box with damp sponge inside, fridge runs at standard cold. Limited to small wheels.
3. **Cool cellar / larder** — if available and stable, the original solution. Hard to find in modern UK houses.
4. **Repurposed mini-fridge with external thermostat** — DIY option. Set to 13 °C, add humidity micro-cave.

A **hygrometer** in the storage space is mandatory. Without one you're guessing.

## Methods compared

There are essentially four ways to manage the surface of an aged cheese. They produce different cheeses; choose deliberately.

### 1. Bandaged (clothbound)

Muslin soaked in lard, coconut oil, or butter, applied tight to the cheese.

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Moisture loss | Moderate, controlled — drives flavour concentration |
| Rind | Develops on the cloth; cheese surface stays under fabric |
| Microbial activity | Multi-coloured moulds welcome on cloth; under-cloth mould = bandage failure |
| Fat infusion | Lard/oil seeps into surface layer over weeks, contributes flavour |
| Maintenance | Weekly turn + inspect; spot-treat lifted bandage; occasional re-oil |
| Humidity range | **75–85 %** — lower end, mould-suppressing |
| Best for | Traditional cheddar, farmhouse-style hard cheeses, anyone wanting "clothbound" character |
| Style result | Drier, flakier, complex, "open" flavour with nutty/earthy notes from the rind ecology |

See [recipes/cheddar.md](recipes/cheddar.md) for application technique and dimensions.

### 2. Waxed

Multiple thin coats of food-grade cheese wax over a dried, brined wheel.

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Moisture loss | Near zero (impermeable barrier) |
| Rind | None — wheel stays at pressing texture |
| Microbial activity | None on outside; any mould under wax indicates wet application |
| Fat infusion | None |
| Maintenance | Minimal — check for cracks every few weeks |
| Humidity range | 65–80 % (less critical — wax is the barrier) |
| Best for | Milder cheeses, longer storage, lower maintenance |
| Style result | Softer texture, milder character, "preserved" rather than evolved |

Application: dip or brush 2–3 thin coats on a fully dried, lightly brined cheese. Surface must be bone dry — moisture under wax is a serious mould risk.

### 3. Natural rind (naked)

Cheese exposed to air; its own surface dries and develops a rind microbiome.

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Moisture loss | Highest of all methods — cheese loses 15–25 % weight |
| Rind | Forms directly on cheese surface as moulds and yeasts colonise |
| Microbial activity | Active surface ecology; managed by wiping, oil rubs, and turning |
| Fat infusion | Optional — natural-rind cheeses sometimes get periodic oil rub |
| Maintenance | **Daily flipping months 1–2**, weekly wipe, occasional oil rub |
| Humidity range | **85–95 %** — high end, mould-encouraging |
| Best for | Alpine styles, large wheels, experienced affineurs |
| Style result | Earthy, complex, "cave-aged" flavour profile; pronounced rind character |

Not recommended for small home wheels (high SA/V loses moisture too fast → cracking) or first-time makers (high maintenance).

### 4. Vacuum-packed

Cheese sealed in a food-grade vacuum bag.

| Aspect | Detail |
|---|---|
| Moisture loss | Near zero |
| Rind | None |
| Microbial activity | Suppressed by oxygen exclusion |
| Fat infusion | None |
| Maintenance | Minimal — inspect through bag monthly for "blowing" or pinking |
| Humidity range | Irrelevant — bag is the barrier |
| Best for | Anyone wanting low effort; block cheddar; supermarket-style |
| Style result | "Stalled" or slowed ageing; softest texture; cleanest flavour; least complex |

Surface must be dried 2–4 days at cave temp before sealing. Moisture in bag = anaerobic off-flavours, sliminess, or "blowing" from gas-producing bacteria.

### Hybrid: bandage then wax

Bandage for the first 6–8 weeks (rind develops, surface dries, fat infuses), then peel and wax for the rest of ageing. Commercial practice ("clothbound then waxed for transport"). Captures most of the bandage's flavour benefits with wax's ongoing low maintenance.

## Decision tree: which method?

```
What do you want from the cheese?

├── Traditional clothbound character, willing to maintain:
│       → Bandage
├── Classic clothbound for first 6–8 weeks, then minimal effort:
│       → Bandage → wax hybrid
├── Mild, smooth, low effort:
│       → Wax
├── Wettest, simplest, supermarket-block style:
│       → Vacuum-pack
├── Earthy, cave-aged complexity, willing to flip daily:
│       → Natural rind (only for large wheels >1.5 kg)
└── Just want to make it through 3 months without binning it:
        → Wax (failsafe for first-time makers)
```

## Maintenance schedules

### Bandaged

| Wheel age | Routine |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | Inspect after 24h, then days 3, 5, 7. Look for bandage settling, weeping, lifts. |
| Weeks 2–4 | Weekly turn, inspect, brine-wipe any cloth mould that gets thick, spot-patch any bandage lifts |
| Months 2–3 | Weekly turn + inspect |
| Months 4–6 | Fortnightly |
| Months 6+ | Fortnightly to monthly; consider tasting cuts |

### Waxed

| Wheel age | Routine |
|---|---|
| Day 1–7 | Check for cracks; re-wax any that appear |
| Weeks 2–4 | Weekly visual check |
| Months 2+ | Monthly check |
| Anytime | If you see condensation or mould under wax, peel and reseal |

### Natural rind

| Wheel age | Routine |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | **Flip daily**, brine-wipe each flip |
| Weeks 2–4 | Flip daily, wipe twice weekly |
| Months 2–3 | Flip every other day, weekly wipe |
| Months 4+ | Weekly flip, optional oil rub if rind drying |

### Vacuum

| Wheel age | Routine |
|---|---|
| Anytime | Monthly visual through bag. If bag swells, liquid pools, or colour shifts to pink/grey — open and inspect immediately |

## Surface management — mould identification and treatment

For bandaged and natural-rind cheeses, the surface is alive with microbial activity throughout ageing. The job is to support beneficial activity while suppressing unwanted moulds.

For **bandaged cheddar specifically**: the lard/oil-soaked muslin is the rind. Surface mould **on the cloth** is normal and expected. Surface mould **under the cloth, against the cheese surface**, indicates the bandage has failed locally — a gap, lift, or thin spot.

### Identifying mould

| Colour / texture | Likely organism | OK for cheddar? | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| White fluffy/cotton | Mucor or *P. candidum* | Mostly benign | Wipe with brine |
| Soft white marshmallow-like | *P. candidum* | Beneficial on Brie/Camembert; unwanted on cheddar | Wipe |
| Blue / blue-green spots | *P. roqueforti* | Beneficial on blue cheese; unwanted on cheddar | Wipe with brine + vinegar |
| Grey/brown "cat hair" tufts | Mucor | Benign | Wipe with brine |
| **Black** | Aspergillus, Cladosporium | **Not OK** — may produce mycotoxins | Brush off, scrape, wash; observe carefully |
| **Pink / orange / red** | Brevibacterium (washed-rind), Rhodotorula yeast, or others | Beneficial only if intentional washed-rind | Wipe with brine + vinegar |
| Green (non-blue) | Various Penicillia, Cladosporium | Unwanted | Wipe with brine + vinegar |
| Yellow patches | Possibly *Geotrichum* or surface yeasts | Sometimes benign | Wipe if not wanted |

Rule of thumb: **white and grey are usually OK; black and pink are warning colours; blue/green are problems on a non-blue cheese**.

### Wash solutions

**Brine (default, mildest):**
- **30 g non-iodised salt in 1 L water** (= 3 % w/v)
- Bring water to boil, dissolve salt, cool completely
- Optionally add **1 tbsp 30 % CaCl₂** for additional calcium-ion stress on mould cells
- Store in a clean jar in the fridge; lasts weeks
- Use on a clean cloth wrung damp (not soaking)

**Brine + vinegar (moderate):**
- Above brine plus **1 tsp white wine vinegar per litre** (~0.5 % v/v)
- Slightly more aggressive — vinegar lowers surface pH and adds organic acid stress
- **Don't use on washed-rind cheeses** — kills the desired *Brevibacterium*

**Salt water + vinegar (aggressive):**
- **30 g salt + 50 mL white wine vinegar in 1 L water** (~5 % v/v vinegar)
- For stubborn or extensive mould
- Cheese will need to air-dry several days afterwards

**CaCl₂ alone (not recommended as primary):**
- Has mild antimicrobial effect via the chloride ion and osmotic stress, but **salt brine is more antimicrobial** at the concentrations typically used
- OK as an emergency wipe if that's what's to hand; switch to brine for ongoing care

### Treatment by severity

| Severity | Action |
|---|---|
| Surface mould on bandage only | Brush off; brine-wipe the cloth surface; re-oil if cracking |
| Mould under bandage, small patches (< 2 cm²) | Lift bandage at affected spots; brine-wipe cheese surface; dry briefly; re-melt oil over area; press flat |
| Mould under bandage, multiple spots or > 4 cm² | Peel affected face's bandage entirely; brine-wash cheese; **air-dry 2–3 days** at cave temp; re-bandage that face with fresh muslin and oil |
| Subsurface lines or pockets, no mould visible inside | Probe with knife at depth — confirm whether mould or air pocket; if just air, seal surface aggressively with oil and observe |
| Subsurface mould penetration confirmed | **Cut a 2.5 cm (1 inch) cone around and below** the affected zone for hard cheese; brine-wash exposed surface; re-bandage |
| Black or pink mould, extensive | Full unwrap; aggressive scrape (knife edge); salt-vinegar wash; air dry 3+ days; consider re-bandage or just consume sooner |
| Off smell — strong ammonia, sulphur, putrid | Inspect carefully — over-ripe is recoverable, contaminated is not; if in doubt, bin |
| > 1/3 surface affected, or mould visible deep inside | **Bin the wheel.** Mycotoxins from deep mould aren't recoverable |

### Specific scenarios

**"I see mould lines or pockets just under the surface"**

Most likely causes:
1. Surface mould pushed in through a bandage gap → cut 2.5 cm around and below; rewash; re-seal
2. Air pockets from imperfect press knitting → not mould; seal surface more aggressively to prevent inwards colonisation
3. Calcium lactate crystals (small white specks/veins) → benign; sign of acid development; appears typically 5+ months
4. Anaerobic "blowing" from gas-producing bacteria → off smell, slimy walls; rare in well-made cheddar; not recoverable

**Probe one suspect spot with a clean knife.** If knife comes out with fuzzy/coloured material → mould penetration. If clean → air pocket.

**"Bandage is lifting / cracking"**

- Small lifts: brush extra melted oil into the gap, press flat
- Multiple lifts on one face: peel that face's bandage entirely, brine-wash, dry, re-bandage with fresh muslin
- Cracks across the bandage: as above — partial re-bandage

**"Mould smell when I open the cave"**

Some mould smell is normal in a cave with multiple cheeses. **Strong, sharp, or unpleasant smells** indicate one of:
- Excessive humidity → reduce sponge, leave lid more cracked
- A cheese is over-developed → identify which and either eat it soon or move to colder storage
- A cheese is contaminated → identify, isolate, decide whether to keep

**"Whole wheel is fuzzy with mould"**

If bandage is doing its job, this is mostly on the cloth and brushes off. **Wash the whole wheel** with brine + vinegar, air-dry 2–3 days, decide whether to:
- Continue ageing with rebuilt bandage (most cases)
- Switch to vacuum pack for the remainder (eliminates further mould issues)
- Switch to wax (also eliminates further mould, harder to undo)

### Prevention

**Pre-bandage (at the cheese):**
- **Dry the cheese surface 2–4 days** at cave temperature before bandaging — applies to natural-rind ageing too. Wet cheese trapped under a bandage is a mould incubator.
- **Apply bandage while the cheese is still slightly warm** from pressing (~25 °C) — rind is tackier, cloth adheres better, gaps are smaller.
- **Pre-cut muslin pieces** to size before melting the fat — avoids fumbling with hot oil and fabric.
- **Apply fat liberally** at the seams and edges; cracks and lifts start at the seam between top circle and side strip.
- **Press lightly** (~1–2 kg) for 30 min after bandaging so fat sets while the cloth is pressed against the cheese.

**Storage hygiene:**
- Don't crowd wheels — air circulation between cheeses prevents one going-bad cheese taking another down with it
- Wash hands before handling
- Separate cloths for separate cheeses if possible
- Don't reuse old bandage for new cheeses — fresh muslin every time
- Clean the cave every few months — wipe interior with mild bleach or vinegar, dry

**When making the cheese:**
- **Adequate salt** (2–2.5 % w/w of milled curd) — salt is your main antimicrobial throughout ageing
- **Don't drain at too-high pH** (>6.4) — more retained lactose can fuel post-drain microbial activity
- **Get the curd properly knit in the press** — air pockets become mould reservoirs
- **Tight bandage** — see prevention list above

### Tools to keep in the cheese kit

- Small jar of brine (3 %), refreshed monthly
- Bottle of white wine vinegar
- Clean cotton cloths or muslin offcuts
- Small saucepan for re-melting oil
- Spare muslin offcuts pre-cut to standard wheel size
- Sharp paring knife
- Optional: small melon baller or apple corer for clean excision of penetration spots
- Thermometer and hygrometer for cave monitoring

## When to taste

The temptation to cut early is strong. Resist for at least:

- **Bandaged hard cheese**: 8 weeks minimum before first taste
- **Waxed hard cheese**: 6 weeks minimum
- **Natural rind**: 10 weeks minimum
- **Vacuum**: 8 weeks minimum

After each tasting cut, **seal the cut face immediately** — wax it, wrap it tightly in cling film, or cover with melted oil + muslin. An exposed cut face dries the rest of the wheel from inside.

For a planned tasting schedule on small home wheels (~700 g), see [recipes/cheddar.md](recipes/cheddar.md) "Maturation by age tier".

## Signs and what they mean — quick diagnostic table

| Observation | Probable cause | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Bandage lifting, dry curling | Cave too dry, or pressed too cold | Patch with oil; raise humidity |
| Bandage moulds blackening | Humidity too high; air stagnant | Increase ventilation; reduce sponge size |
| Pink/orange tinge on rind or under cloth | *Brevibacterium* or Rhodotorula yeast | Wipe brine + vinegar; isolate from other wheels |
| Wheel weeping fat | Pressed too warm, cave too warm, or oversalted | Reduce cave temp; for next batch, hit 22 °C pre-press |
| Wheel cracking | Humidity too low, over-aged, or under-salted | Patch cracks with oil/wax; raise humidity |
| Calcium lactate crystals (white specks/veins) | Acid development on track | Benign; sign of good ageing |
| Strong ammonia smell | Over-ripe or contaminated | Inspect: over-ripe is recoverable, contaminated isn't |
| Bag swelling (vacuum) | Gas-producing bacteria active | Open immediately; smell test; usually not recoverable |
| Tracks or veins of mould inside cheese | Surface mould has penetrated; pressed with internal air pockets | Cut 2.5 cm around and below (see Treatment by severity) |

## Common home-affineur mistakes

1. **Humidity too high for bandaged style** — 85+ % invites surface mould. Aim 75–80 %.
2. **Cave temperature cycling** — fridge defrost cycles cause condensation. Move wheel to a stable spot.
3. **Bandage applied to wet cheese** — moisture trapped under cloth = mould. Dry 2–4 days first.
4. **Skipping the turn** — moisture pools at the down face, encourages rot on that side. Weekly turns are non-negotiable.
5. **Tasting too early** — under 6 weeks tells you nothing; you've just damaged the rind for no info gain.
6. **Sealing tasting cuts too late** — the exposed face dries within hours, ruining the rest.
7. **Over-treating with vinegar** — repeated vinegar wash inhibits even beneficial organisms; use only when needed.
8. **Trying to age past the practical window** — small home wheels peak at 5–7 months, not 12+. SA/V scaling matters.

## Long-term planning

A working home affinage routine looks like:

- **Make day 0** — into press
- **Day 1** — out of press, brine or dry-salt, dry-rest
- **Day 2–4** — surface dry (and brined if natural rind)
- **Day 5–7** — bandage/wax/vacuum/dry on rack
- **Weeks 1–8** — frequent maintenance, rind establishment
- **Months 2–6** — settle into the chosen rhythm
- **Month 3 onward** — start tasting cuts
- **Month 5–7** — peak window for small home wheels
- **Month 7+** — quality typically falling for small wheels

Multiple wheels at different ages in the same cave teach you the most. A rolling schedule of 4–6 wheels at staggered ages means you always have one ready to eat.
